# 1. Recruitment Criteria

For the $\sim 15\%$ of peers stuck behind dual Symmetric NATs or enterprise firewalls where direct hole punching mathematically fails, the protocol recruits **Emergent Relayers** from within the swarm. This replaces expensive central TURN servers with a crowd-sourced model.

A node is recruited as an Emergent Relayer if and only if it satisfies the following strict criteria:
1.  **Direct Accessibility:** `Reachability = PUBLIC` (Ch2 §2.1.3) — no NAT or full cone.
2.  **Symmetrical Bandwidth:** Symmetrical upload capacity $u_{\text{relay}} \ge 20\text{ Mbps}$.
3.  **High Stability:** Rolling uptime $\ge 30\text{ minutes}$ and a packet drop rate $\le 0.5\%$.

```text
   [ Symmetric Peer A ] <---> [ Public Superpeer (Relay) ] <---> [ Cone Parent B ]
```

**What a relay bridges, and what it does not.** A `SYMMETRIC` peer is leaf-class (Ch1 §1.2.5) and connects *outbound* to `PUBLIC` parents without help; the relay exists for the cases that remain — a needed tree whose only reachable parents are `CONE`, and the leaf's own PULL contribution, which a peer nobody can reach inbound could not otherwise make. The relay bridges media in both directions between A and B: B's tree push to A, and A's `PULL_REQUEST` service to peers that ask it. The protocol has no "relayed relay": a NAT-blocked node never holds tree children of its own through a relay, so no slot accounting has to be split across two machines.

**A bridge is a tree edge, twice.** For tree $T_m$ the relay $R$ is an ordinary **child of $B$**: it joins $B$ with `NEIGHBOR(TreeID = m)` and its own `RANK_PROOF`, occupies one of $B$'s $K_B(m)$ slots, decodes, verifies and re-encodes as every interior node does (Ch4 §4.1.2), and issues $B$ ordinary `PROOF_OF_UPLOAD` receipts. $A$ is then an ordinary **child of $R$** in $T_m$: it receives $R$'s pushed stripe, and issues $R$ receipts with `RxFlags.RELAYED` (§6.3.2). Nothing at $B$ is special — an earlier draft left it open whether $R$ or $A$ was $B$'s child, under which reading $B$ received no receipts for the slot it served and evicted the bridge after three segments. $R$ has out-degree one in a tree it is not assigned to; this is the single bounded exception to the Orthogonal Placement Rule (Ch1 §1.2.1), and $R$ does **not** set bit $m$ in its `AssignedTrees` for a bridged tree, so it is never elected Deputy for it. $A$'s hop depth is $B$'s plus two. A peer bridged through one relay in several trees has correlated parents and marks them so (Ch1 §1.2.1 *Distinct-Parent Rule*); it prefers distinct relays per tree where the sample offers them.

**Advertisement and discovery.** A qualifying node sets `Flags.RELAY_CAPABLE` in its registration (it is exempt from most of the registration sampling, Ch2 §2.3.2) and in every Peer Record about it. A peer needing a relay issues `GET_PEERS` with `ReqFlags.WANT_RELAY_CAPABLE` and receives a random sample of them; it then sends `RELAY_PROPOSAL` (§6.3.3), naming the parent $B$ it could not reach or leaving the choice to $R$.

**Cost accounting.** A bridged child costs $R$ exactly what a tree child costs, $B_m \Omega$ of upload, and is charged to the same place: the **tree budget** $(1 - r_{\text{pull}})\,u_R$, under the owning expression of Ch1 §1.2.1 *The Two Budgets*. Bridged load $U_{\text{bridge}}$ may take at most half of that budget, and $R$'s slot count in its own assigned trees is computed with $U_{\text{bridge}}$ removed. $R$ accepts a `RELAY_PROPOSAL` only while $U_{\text{bridge}} + B_m\Omega \le \frac{1}{2}(1 - r_{\text{pull}})u_R$ **and** it can obtain a slot at some parent for $T_m$. An earlier draft charged bridges to the PULL reserve $r_{\text{pull}} u_R$ and said they "never touch its tree slots". On the $20$ Mbps node these criteria admit, that reserve is $2$ Mbps: two $0.75$ Mbps stripes at $M = 6$, one $1.5$ Mbps stripe, and **no** $3.0$ Mbps tree — the whole stream at $M = 2$, the top layer at $M = 3$ — so the home broadcaster behind CGNAT, "a primary use case at $N = 2$–$10$" (§6.3.3), could bind no ingress relay and no NAT-blocked viewer of a small stream could be bridged. A bridged child is a subscriber served, not capacity lost to the forest; charging it to the reserve made the bridge unaffordable exactly where it was needed. The half-budget cap is what still bounds it: at $15\%$ of $10^6$ peers behind symmetric NAT, only the fraction that cannot find a `PUBLIC` parent needs a bridge, and no relay can turn more than half of itself into one.
