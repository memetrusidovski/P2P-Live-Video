A tokenless, trackerless live-video protocol

BitStream

One broadcaster, up to a million viewers, no CDN. The stream is cut into 250 ms signed chunks, split across a forest of up to six trees, and carried by the viewers' own uplinks. Every byte is verified against the broadcaster's signature before it is forwarded.

This page walks the algorithm as the specification in protocol/ defines it. Every number is the spec's own. Sliders and diagrams recompute the spec's formulas live.

1

The problem: a stream costs the sender N times its bitrate

A CDN pays for every viewer. A swarm pays nothing extra per viewer, but only if the viewers' uploads add up.

A 6 Mbps stream to a million viewers is 6 Tbps of egress from a CDN. BitStream makes the viewers the edge. That only works when the swarm can carry itself, and the spec writes that condition with the overheads it actually pays:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{N} (1 - r_{\text{pull}})\, u_i \;\ge\; \Omega \cdot N \cdot B$$

\(u_i\) is a viewer's upload, \(B\) the stream bitrate, \(\Omega \approx 1.15\) the parity and framing overhead on every forwarded byte, and \(r_{\text{pull}} = 0.10\) the slice of upload every node holds back for repair traffic. The naive form \(\sum u_i \ge N B\) understates the requirement by about 28 %.

Two bars matter, and they differ. Mean upload above about 7.7 Mbps makes full quality deliverable. Mean upload above about 10.2 Mbps also buys a fan-out of 8, which is what the depth proof of section 2 assumes. The spec treats failure of the first bar as the common case, not a corner, and defines what happens then (section 10).

2

One chunk's journey, and where the four seconds go

The encoder produces 1 s closed-GOP segments. Each segment is cut into four 250 ms chunks. The broadcaster hashes each chunk's 16 KB blocks into a Blake3 Merkle tree, signs the root in a MANIFEST, and starts pushing the moment the signature exists.

The depth bound comes from fan-out. With each relay feeding \(k = 8\) children, a million nodes fit in \(D = \log_8(7{,}000{,}001) - 1 \approx 6.6\) levels, so the forest caps depth at \(D_{\max} = 8\) and a parent refuses any join that would go deeper.

3

Identity and discovery: who you are, where the stream is

There is no tracker. Peers find each other through an S/Kademlia distributed hash table. A stream is named by the hash of the broadcaster's Ed25519 public key, so a stream record is self-authenticating: whoever serves it, only the broadcaster can have signed it.

Two proof-of-work puzzles

A node identity is a keypair plus two nonces. The static puzzle is paid once per identity, the dynamic one every time the public address changes. The spec is explicit that these make identities rate-limited, not expensive. The Sybil bound is address diversity, enforced by per-prefix caps wherever identities are counted.

$$\text{Blake3}(PK \parallel N_{\text{static}}) \le 2^{256 - C_1}, \qquad \text{Blake3}\big(\text{NodeID} \parallel IP \parallel N_{\text{dyn}}\big) \le 2^{256 - C_2}$$

Receivers verify the dynamic puzzle against the address the packet actually arrived from. That is why a peer cannot declare its own address, and why a node that bootstraps through 127.0.0.1 while advertising a LAN address is rejected by every peer it talks to.

Registration that stays cheap at a million viewers

Twenty DHT nodes nearest the stream key act as guardians. Registration is sampled: a peer registers with probability \(2^{-s}\) where \(s = \lceil \log_2(N / 10^4) \rceil\), so guardian load is bounded whatever N is. Registrations live 180 s and are refreshed every 90 s. A record counts toward the swarm size only if refreshed within 135 s, so departed peers stop inflating counts within one refresh period.

Each guardian also counts, per tree, the peers that re-queried it with a starved bit in the last 10 s. The publisher reads those counts on every record store and uses them as its only global signal (section 10).

The join handshake

4

The multi-forest: split the stream so no home uplink carries all of it

A 10 Mbps uplink can feed one child at 6 Mbps, or eight children at 0.75 Mbps. The forest is how the second happens.

The stream is encoded as scalable layers: a 480p base layer at 1.5 Mbps, a 720p enhancement at 1.5 Mbps and a 1080p enhancement at 3.0 Mbps on the reference ladder. The source stripes those layers over \(M\) trees. A relay is an interior node in exactly one tree, chosen by a rendezvous hash of its own identity, and a leaf in every other tree. So a relay uploads one stripe to its children and downloads everything else.

How many trees, and what each one carries

\(M\) grows with the number of relay-class peers, from 2 up to 6. Too many trees at small N would leave some tree with no relay at all. The source maps layers to trees by a minimax rule: give every layer one tree, then hand each spare tree to the layer with the largest per-tree bitrate, ties to the lower layer. The largest stripe is then as small as M allows, and the trees carrying lower layers are never harder to relay than the trees carrying higher ones.

Slots: what one uplink can promise

A relay's slot count in its tree is computed against that tree's own bitrate, with the overhead and the PULL reserve removed first:

$$K_v(m) = \left\lfloor \frac{(1 - r_{\text{pull}})\, u_v}{t_v \cdot B_m \cdot \Omega_v} \right\rfloor, \qquad \Omega_v = \Big(1 + \frac{\bar E_v}{16}\Big)(1 + 0.085)$$
A relay's upload is available only to the layer its tree carries. Idle slots on a 1080p tree cannot feed a starving base-layer tree. Only the source can change the mapping, and section 10 is about when it does.
5

Choosing a parent: capacity, latency and depth in one unit

A joiner probes every candidate that relays the tree it wants and scores each one in milliseconds, so the three terms are directly comparable:

$$\text{Score}(p) = 12 \cdot \min\!\big(\sqrt{K_{\text{avail}}},\ 16\big)\, R \;-\; RTT(p) \;-\; 20\,\big(e^{0.5\,h_p} - 1\big)$$

Capacity credit saturates at 256 free slots (192 ms). RTT enters at unit weight. The depth term is exponential, so depth dominates everything: a parent at depth 7 costs 643 ms of penalty before its RTT is counted.

Who gets the slot when the tree is full

A join carries the joiner's rank: full-stream-equivalents it is currently delivering to others, proven from signed receipts (section 11). The parent decides in order:

  1. Free slot: accepted.
  2. Enhancement tree, no slot, joiner's rank above 1.25× the lowest child's: accepted; that child is drained out over 5 segments while still being served.
  3. Base-layer tree, no slot, joiner is leaf-class, leaves hold less than 20 % of the slots: accepted; the lowest-ranked relay-class child that does not itself relay this tree is drained out. Nobody is orphaned.
  4. Base-layer tree otherwise: refused. Base-layer slots are never taken from a sitting child by rank.

Two things follow. A new relay bootstraps through the base layer: it ranks zero, takes a free base-layer slot, forwards, earns receipts, and within a minute can preempt into enhancement trees. And contributors drift toward the source: every 5 s a node re-scores its parents and migrates up when a candidate beats the current one by a hysteresis margin, which is halved in the shallower direction so one hop of depth always wins.

6

A peer's lifecycle

Every node runs one state machine. Repair is a per-tree sub-state of ACTIVE, not a node state: an earlier draft stopped a node forwarding every tree while it repaired one, and a single dead parent became a churn wave down every subtree.

The join threshold adapts to the swarm: \(\theta_{\text{join}} = \max(1, \min(4, N - 1))\). At N = 2 the first viewer connects straight to the source; at N ≥ 5 it waits for four active-set peers. A fixed threshold of 4 would have blocked every stream at N < 5 forever.

7

Losing a parent: detection, election, re-attach, all scaled by RTT

Media arrives in bursts, so silence alone proves nothing. A parent that has sent nothing for 100 ms sends a heartbeat. A child that sees 100 ms of silence probes. The eviction deadline is taken from the connection's own QUIC RTT estimator, so a far parent is not declared dead on its first lost heartbeat:

$$\tau_{\text{evict}} = \max\big(200\text{ ms},\ 2\tau_{\text{ping}} + SRTT + 4\,RTTVAR\big)$$

Siblings elect a deputy without talking to each other

When a relay dies, its children in that tree are orphaned together. Each already holds the same per-tree ROSTER its parent sent every second: the top 8 children that relay this tree, ordered by free slots, ties by lowest NodeID. Every orphan computes the same deputy from the same snapshot. The deputy finds its own new parent from its passive set; the other orphans send it a high-priority join and wait 45 ms before falling back to the ordinary path. The subtree re-attaches as a unit instead of a thundering herd.

8

Blocks, proofs and parity: verify before you forward

Each 250 ms chunk is cut, layer by layer, into 16 KB blocks. The blocks are leaves of a Blake3 Merkle tree whose root the broadcaster signs. A block travels with its sibling hashes, so any node can check it against the signed root in microseconds and forward it only then. Corrupt data cannot travel more than one hop.

Loss is repaired on the link, not by asking again

Each block is one RaptorQ source block: 16 symbols of 1024 bytes plus \(E\) parity symbols sized per child link from the loss rate \(\rho\) the child reports in its receipt every second. The first 16 symbols are the raw bytes, so on a clean link a relay reconstructs the block by concatenation and never runs the decoder.

\(E\) is the smallest parity count that leaves at most one block in a thousand per hop for the PULL path, between one symbol and the 30 % ceiling:

$$E = \min\Big\{ e \in [1,\ \lceil 0.30K \rceil] \;:\; \Pr\big[\text{Bin}(K + e,\ \rho) > e\big] \le 10^{-3} \Big\}, \qquad K = 16$$

The verification boundary is the block, never the symbol. A relay does decode → verify → re-encode → push at 16 KB granularity. Receipts (section 11) attest verified blocks, one per segment per tree with a bitmap of the blocks verified.

9

The playout buffer: push at the edge, pull before the deadline

A peer plays 3.0 s behind the live edge it receives. The newest part of the buffer is filled by the tree push with no requests at all. The oldest part, the PULL zone, is where a peer asks neighbours for anything still missing, by block, from mesh neighbours in other branches. The zone is sized to cover one parent repair plus two PULL round trips on the peer's own paths:

$$W_{\text{pull}} = \text{clamp}\big(\tau_{\text{evict}}^{\max} + 6\,SRTT^{\max},\ 1.5\text{ s},\ 2.0\text{ s}\big)$$

Joining mid-stream

The Stream Record names the live edge, but that hint is up to about 1.7 s stale by the time a joiner acts on it. So the record is a bootstrap hint only. The peer re-anchors on the highest segment actually arriving on the push path once it is ACTIVE, and never moves the anchor backward. Two viewers in the same room converge on the same offset however slow their lookups were. Every peer retains 8 s of verified segments so a joiner can backfill segment X from a neighbour while segment X+1 is already being pushed to it.

10

When upload runs short: shed, fold, reserve

The sustainability condition fails often on real residential links. The spec's answer is resolution loss, never a frozen player.

Shortage shows up locally, as a failed join: every candidate for a tree answers REJECTED_SATURATED or sits at the depth limit. After two consecutive failed rounds in any tree of a layer, the peer sheds that layer and everything above it, renders the layer below, and does not retry for 10 s. Rounds in which some candidate was still warming up do not count, so a growth burst is never mistaken for a shortage. The base layer is never shed.

Shedding cannot move capacity. Folding can.

A relay's slots are in its assigned tree, and that tree carries whatever layer the source put there. When the base layer starves at M = 6 with 4 Mbps relays, peers shedding 1080p free nothing for 480p: a third of the relays sit on 1080p stripes nobody can afford. Only the source can re-map, and it already has the signal: the guardians' per-tree starved counts. If a tree of a lower layer shows more than 5 % of the swarm starved on average over 10 s (base layer) or 30 s (others), the source emits a MANIFEST_UPDATE with the same M, one fewer layer, and the mapping re-run. No relay moves; no remaining tree's bitrate rises; slot counts rise. The base layer re-striped over three trees at 0.5 Mbps is exactly sustainable where (2,2,2) left one peer in three without it.

Unfolding is gated on the relay count growing by 50 % since the fold, never on a timer: a wrong unfold would re-create the freeze that caused the fold.

The last resort costs the broadcaster a constant

The source holds back \(R_{\text{src}} = 3 \cdot b_0 \cdot \Omega \approx 5.2\) Mbps, three full copies of the base layer, as an emergency pool that serves base-layer slots directly when the starved counts say no mapping can carry them. That is the whole worst case: 480p, partly source-fed, at a cost that never grows with N.

11

Receipts and rank: contribution buys placement

Every child signs one receipt per segment per tree for the blocks it verified from its parent. A relay's rank is the throughput those receipts prove over the last 60 segments, divided by the stream bitrate: how many full streams it is currently delivering to others. Receipts are capped at what one segment of the named tree can honestly contain, so declaring bytes buys nothing.

$$\Theta^{\text{rate}}_A = \frac{1}{60\text{ s}} \sum_{\text{last 60 segments}} w_j \cdot \min\big(\text{Bytes}_j,\ \text{Cap}_j\big) \cdot 8, \qquad \text{rank} = \Theta^{\text{rate}}_A / B$$

Proving rank in a few kilobytes

A super node holds half a million receipts a minute, and no verifier would trust a self-reported total. So rank is presented as a commit-then-sample proof. The prover sorts its receipts by a canonical key, commits to a Merkle root and a count, and the sample is chosen by the root of a manifest the broadcaster signed after the commitment, which the prover could not have known. Eight adjacent pairs are opened. Each pair must be in strict key order, which is what makes the count a count of distinct receipts: a duplicate must sit next to its original in sorted order and the pair check catches it.

The proof is about 6 to 10 KB and is sent once per admission attempt, never per block.

What rank buys, and what it cannot touch

Rank buys
  • enhancement-layer slots, by preempting a child ranked below 1/1.25 of the joiner
  • shallower placement, through the 5 s migration loop
  • interior status in more trees, one per full stream delivered
  • standing to accuse (rank ≥ 0.25)
Rank never touches
  • a sitting child's base-layer slot
  • the leaf share: 20 % of base-layer slots a leaf can claim by displacing a pure subscriber
  • repair traffic, which Tit-for-Tat governs separately over the 10 % PULL reserve in 500 ms cycles

Leaf-class devices, phones on cellular for instance, never upload and rank zero. They receive the base layer by right and pay in quality ceiling: they are the first child preempted when an enhancement tree is contended. The spec states the residual honestly: a colluding uploader with Sybil downloaders can forge rank at a cost of identities, not bandwidth, so everything rank buys is bounded independently by the prefix cap and by children that observe non-delivery and leave.

NATs and emergent relays

Reachability is measured at bootstrap from two PONG reflections and decides what a node may be:

ReachabilityNATMay relay?How it is reached
PUBLICnone or full coneyesprobed directly
CONErestricted, port-restrictedyesa PUNCH_REQUEST relayed through whoever introduced it, then the probe; one extra RTT, 1 s validity
SYMMETRICsymmetric, CGNATno, leaf classconnects outbound to PUBLIC parents; bridged by an emergent relay where it needs a CONE parent or wants to contribute

Status of the reference implementation: reflection decides the NAT class, but a node still advertises the address it was configured with, so viewers behind a NAT need a VPN or an explicit public address today. See src/docs/MULTI_MACHINE.md.
